CASE 001  ·  THE PAPERS

The Missing Prom

You see it in Hollywood movies: extravagant ball nights, dresses, tuxedos, limousines, prom kings and queens. But in India, prom never became a tradition. Why?

Culture·Society·Human Behavior

THE FIRST CLUE

Prom originated in the US in the late 19th century as a middle-class alternative to elite debutante balls in Europe, initially as an evening tea. It later became a major milestone for teenagers, giving rise to the modern, extravagant era as the economy boomed after World War II.

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, India, on the other hand, was under British colonial rule—a period characterized by intense economic exploitation and socio-religious reform movements. Youth culture was shaped by survival, community obligation, and the nationalist movement.

Indian society was also highly stratified, divided by deep caste structures, facing severe social issues, and shaped by strong expectations around gender segregation.

THE SECOND CLUE

In traditional Hindu philosophy, life is divided into stages. The first stage is Brahmacharya (student life), which emphasizes discipline and a focus on education and character building. Romance or sexual distraction during this phase was often viewed as inconsistent with those ideals.

In traditional Indian society, youth was not generally viewed as a time for personal exploration or romance. Instead, it was often treated as a transition period managed by the family to protect social standing and community order.

THE THIRD CLUE

Prom culture is a coming-of-age ritual that can include promposals—asking someone to attend with you, often in a romantic context. In India, many parents direct significant financial resources toward their children's education and career prospects. As a result, dating may be seen by some families as an unnecessary distraction.

This is one reason school farewells can serve a different role. Proms can be expensive, and they are not necessarily the milestone by which a person is recognized as a mature adult by society. In many Indian communities, marriage has traditionally held that role.

THE VERDICT

Prom never took root in India in the same way because the social and historical conditions that shaped it were different. It grew in a society where youth milestones such as romance, independence, and peer social rituals became culturally prominent. India's story followed a different path.

Even today, the economics may not add up for many families. When families prioritize education and career expenses, a high-cost social event built around dating and romance can feel unnecessary.

And fundamentally, India already has its own coming-of-age milestones. In many communities, marriage has traditionally marked a major transition into adulthood.

Prom didn't fail in India. It was never invited in.
